Predicting Declining Content from Search Performance Signals

A decision-support analysis using anonymized FlyRank search-performance data

Abstract

This study asks whether search-performance and content-freshness signals can identify content items that are likely to decline, so content teams can prioritize which items should be reviewed first. Using anonymized FlyRank internship warehouse data, March 2026 performance signals were used to construct a prediction snapshot and April 2026 performance was used only to define the future outcome. A decision-tree classifier was evaluated against a previously established rule-based baseline using a stratified held-out test set. The model achieved 0.819 accuracy, 0.680 precision, 0.647 recall, and 0.663 F1 on the held-out sample, compared with baseline precision of 0.495, recall of 0.533, and F1 of 0.513. The results provide a directional, decision-support signal for prioritizing content review rather than evidence of causality or guaranteed future performance.

1. Introduction / Problem Statement

The practical question is whether content teams can use search-performance signals available at a fixed point in time to identify content items that are likely to decline in the following period.

The decision supported by this analysis is content prioritization: which content items should be reviewed first based on their predicted likelihood of decline.

The goal is not to prove why a page declines or to claim that refreshing a page will cause its performance to improve. Instead, the analysis tests whether measurable signals can provide a repeatable way to prioritize review work.

2. Data

The analysis uses the FlyRank internship warehouse release and focuses on anonymized content-performance data. The main source is the daily content-performance fact table, which contains search and traffic performance measures at the client-content-date level.

March 2026 daily performance data was used to construct the March 31, 2026 prediction snapshot. April 2026 daily performance data was used only to construct the future outcome label.

The final modeling population contains 331,436 unique client-content observations present at the March 31 prediction snapshot and having the required April outcome period.

The outcome label identifies an item as declining when its April search impressions were more than 20% lower than the preceding 30-day comparison period, provided the preceding period had positive impressions.

The model uses performance signals available by the March 31 snapshot, including impressions, clicks, average search position, pageviews, sessions, users, and recent 7-day activity.

Current content metadata with timestamps after the prediction snapshot was excluded from the final feature set to reduce the risk of using information that would not have been available at prediction time.

No client names, private URLs, search queries, credentials, or other identifying information are included in the paper outputs.

3. Methodology

Prediction setup

Each observation represents one content item for one client at the March 31, 2026 prediction snapshot.

Features

The feature set contains 11 performance signals derived from March data, including 30-day impressions, clicks, average position, pageviews, sessions, users, 7-day impressions, 7-day clicks, 7-day average position, recent-impression share, and recent-click share.

Label

An item is labelled as declining when April impressions are below 80% of the preceding comparison-period impressions, provided the preceding period had positive impressions.

Label distribution:
331,436 total observations
240,262 non-declining
91,174 declining
Declining rate: 27.51%

Validation

The data was split into training and held-out test sets using an 80/20 stratified split with random state 42. The decline rate was preserved across the two samples.

The model was a Decision Tree Classifier with maximum depth 5. Performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1, and a confusion matrix.

Leakage checks were applied by constructing features from information available by the March 31 prediction snapshot and reserving April data for the future outcome.

On the held-out test set, the model produced 42,506 true negatives, 5,547 false positives, 6,441 false negatives, and 11,794 true positives.

The largest measured feature importance was 30-day impressions at 0.775, followed by recent-impression share at 0.200. The remaining features had substantially smaller measured importance in this fitted tree.

These importance values describe this fitted model and dataset. They should not be interpreted as causal effects or proof that impressions independently cause future decline.

5. Limitations & Honest Framing

  • The outcome is defined using a threshold on future impressions and is therefore a constructed prediction target rather than a direct measurement of content quality.
  • The validation uses a held-out stratified sample rather than a full production-time deployment evaluation.
  • The model is a relatively simple decision tree and may not capture more complex relationships.
  • Feature importance is model-specific and should not be interpreted as causal evidence.
  • The analysis covers the March-to-April 2026 observation window and may not generalize to other periods or populations.
  • The results are observed and directional. They support content-review prioritization but do not establish that refreshing content will cause improved search performance.

Action playbook

  1. Review first: high predicted decline probability combined with substantial recent search impressions.
  2. Investigate the signal: examine recent performance movement and determine whether the item warrants editorial, technical, or metadata review.
  3. Prioritize carefully: use the ranking as a queue for human review rather than an automatic refresh command.
  4. Monitor after action: future performance should be measured separately to determine whether the observed trend changes.
